# Wireless spectrum: market reference and probabilistic outlook

**The thesis is half right. US spectrum already carries real scarcity value, low-band and satellite-capable spectrum is getting scarcer, and SpaceX has become a deep-pocketed buyer that pushed private prices up in 2025-26. But licensed spectrum as a whole is unlikely to become significantly more valuable by 2030: we put 14% on a broad scarcity premium. Traffic growth is slowing, AI is still a sliver of traffic, and a statutory pipeline adds about a third more licensed spectrum by 2030. We expect the 2027 upper C-band auction to draw about $0.77 per MHz-pop, below the $0.87 the 2021 lower C-band fetched on the same population basis, and we see no clean passive way to own the theme.**

Prepared for Fulton Asset Management by Whitesail Labs, November 2026. The interactive report has the charts, market map, forecast panel and full source list; this file holds the text. It is a market reference, not investment advice.

Units: a MHz-pop is one megahertz of bandwidth covering one person, so a nationwide 10 MHz licence is about 3.35 billion MHz-pops, and prices per MHz-pop let bands of different width and reach be compared. An exabyte (EB) is a billion gigabytes. Uplink is data sent from a device to the network.

## Our verdict

**Where the thesis is right**

Some spectrum is scarce, AI strains the uplink, and convergence has brought a new buyer:
- Low-band spectrum (under 1 GHz) carries signals indoors, and nothing new is coming. SpaceX agreed to pay a reported $1.73 per MHz-pop for 800 MHz licences that changed hands at about $0.78 two months earlier.
- SpaceX accounts for about $28B, roughly half, of 2025-26 US private spectrum deal value, and Amazon is paying about $11.6B for Globalstar. SpaceX's IPO raised $75B ($85.7B with the over-allotment) and it pays partly in its own stock, so it could fund the whole upper C-band auction at our $41B central estimate. Its appetite, not its balance sheet, limits further buying.
- Satellites cannot carry city traffic. By our order-of-magnitude estimate, next-generation satellite-to-phone service reaches about 1-6 Mbps per square kilometre, roughly a thousandth of a dense urban network's 1,500-9,000. That is why SpaceX is buying ground-grade spectrum and looking for towers, the convergence the thesis describes.
- AI strains the uplink first. Generative AI traffic was 26% uplink against about 10% for all traffic, phones transmit with limited power, and networks are built for roughly 10:1 download-to-upload. Ericsson models AI lifting 2031 uplink to 3-5x the 2025 level, which favours low- and mid-band coverage spectrum. Tripling uplink adds only about 20% to total traffic, so the pressure falls on particular bands and busy cells more than on total volume.
- Sellers facing two motivated buyers captured large gains. EchoStar's shares rose about 430% from a near-distressed level in January 2025 to June 2026 as AT&T and SpaceX split its licences, though over 2008-2026 Dish's licence spending returned about 1.4x cash cost at deal values. Small paired lots also still clear high at auction: leftover AWS-3 licences fetched $2.53 per MHz-pop in June 2026, almost all from Verizon.

**Where it breaks**

The broader reading, that licensed spectrum as a whole becomes significantly more valuable by 2030, runs into seven problems:
- Demand is slowing. US traffic grew 20% in 2025 after 32% in 2024. Generative AI apps were 0.06% of traffic on the one operator network Ericsson measured in April 2025, and Wi-Fi carries 82-89% of US smartphone data. The devices the thesis names add little licensed traffic so far. Waymo's roughly 4,000 robotaxis use about 0.02% of US mobile traffic, and glasses and most robots ride a phone or Wi-Fi.
- Capacity has substitutes. Wi-Fi carries most phone data, and denser cells and 5G-Advanced radios (20-40% more bits per hertz) add capacity without new licences. Networks also have room today. Capital spending has been flat near $30B since 2023 while speeds rose 74% in two years, though much of that gain came from C-band and 3.45 GHz spectrum bought in 2021-22.
- Supply is rising by law. The 2025 budget law created an 800 MHz pipeline, and we expect about 370 MHz of new spectrum auctioned by 2030.
- SpaceX may be nearly done buying. Musk called the Grain 800 MHz licences the last critical piece for complete US phone coverage. We expect SpaceX to own about 88 MHz at end-2030, about 9 MHz more than it has already agreed to buy.
- Owners rarely capture it. AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile carry about $411B of licences at cost (T-Mobile's $100B is from its filing; AT&T's $149B and Verizon's $161B could not be independently confirmed), 41-50% of their enterprise value. Their shares fell 9-13% the day after SpaceX agreed to buy Grain's 800 MHz licences. The pricing-power objection holds: carriers see stock value from spectrum only if they can raise prices, and competition, now including SpaceX, works against that.
- Buyers are few and regulated. The three carriers took 96% of 2021 C-band dollars and about 99% of the 2026 AWS-3 re-auction. The last outside bidder of size, Dish (about a third of 3.45 GHz in 2022), has sold out, and FCC screens and conditions limit who can hold how much.
- History is unkind to pure plays. Of 16 companies whose main asset was spectrum, 3 clearly won, 8 lost and 5 were mixed. The sample is small and hand-picked.

**What our forecasts say**

Central estimates with 80% ranges, from five forecasting lenses that worked from the same research:
- Upper C-band auction price (2027): $0.77 (80% range $0.42 to $1.35), against 2021's $0.87 on the same basis ($0.94 as originally reported). Chance gross bids exceed $0.94: 31%; chance they beat 2021's $0.87 like-for-like: about 37%. With about $0.30 of clearing and altimeter costs on top, the all-in price is roughly level with 2021's (about $1.01 including its clearing payments).
- Low- and mid-band deal price in 2029-30 (FCC auctions plus private sales above $1B): $0.90 (80% range $0.40 to $1.83), against a median near $1.20 on the same basis in 2025-26. Most of the fall comes from new FCC auctions (2.7 GHz and others, at about $0.3-0.8) entering the mix; we expect private low-band and paired sales to hold at $1.2-2.5.
- US mobile traffic in 2030: 328 EB (80% range 260 EB to 420 EB), about 15.5% a year.
- AI share of mobile traffic in 2030, on a narrow definition like Ericsson's (assistants, agents and AI devices, not AI-recommended video): 1.5% (80% range 0.4% to 8.7%). Broad-definition forecasts run higher, such as GSMA's 5-20%.
- Spectrum SpaceX owns at end-2030: 88 MHz (80% range 66 MHz to 150 MHz). Chance SpaceX sells its own US phone plan by 2028: 53%.
- US wireless service revenue in 2030: $266B (80% range $246B to $285B); revenue per GB $0.80 (80% range $0.63 to $1.04).
- Chance an equal-weight basket of AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile beats the S&P 500 through 2030: 36%.
Scenarios for 2030: Steady grind 39%; Supply wave 24%; SpaceX fourth force 23%; Scarcity premium 14%. The thesis holds in about 37% of them: 14% where existing holders gain (Scarcity premium) and 23% where SpaceX and sellers gain (SpaceX fourth force). The demand case is stronger for 2030-2035, mainly through AI uplink.

**Ways to get exposure**

No listed security gives clean, passive exposure to spectrum values. The options trade purity against liquidity:
- US carriers (T, VZ, TMUS) own most of the spectrum. Their licences sit on the books at cost, equal to 41-50% of enterprise value, and higher spectrum prices reach earnings only if they sell. Their returns follow pricing and competition.
- Small spectrum holders (Anterix, NextNav, Array) are the purest listed exposure; EchoStar's remaining licences come only through ECHO stock. These holdings pay off through a sale to a handful of buyers, so the exposure is event-driven and illiquid. We put 79% on at least one of the four selling by 2028, but most of that is EchoStar's promise to the FCC to sell its remaining licences by September 2028. For Anterix, NextNav or Array the chance is roughly one in three, and a sale does not guarantee a premium.
- EchoStar is now mostly a claim on SpaceX shares (about $36B at the 13 November close) and cash from its spectrum sales. At about $28.6B it trades well below that look-through value, before taxes and wind-down costs.
- SpaceX (SPCX) is the most active buyer, but spectrum is about 1.5% of its value.
- Globalstar and Iridium are being acquired at fixed prices, which caps their upside. AST SpaceMobile is a high-risk bet on execution.
- Tower companies hold no spectrum but gain if scarcity forces more sites or SpaceX leases them.
- No fund isolates spectrum. IYZ is led by the large carriers; XTL gives equal weight to carriers, equipment makers and small spectrum and satellite names.
If convergence plays out, the evidence points to gains for sellers and for SpaceX as buyer, while incumbent carriers lose ground. Those gains come through deals, so the exposure is event-driven.

**What would change our view**

Signposts that would move us toward the thesis, with our current odds:
- The upper C-band auction's gross bids exceed $0.94 per MHz-pop (31%), or SpaceX wins meaningful blocks in it, not just rural licences (part of our 45% for any win).
- US traffic growth returns above 25% in any year to 2030 (14%), or carriers raise real prices while rationing fixed wireless for capacity. A rise in revenue per GB (7%) would point the same way, unless it comes from a change in CTIA's method.
- A technology company agrees to buy a US carrier or a cable company's mobile business by 2030 (16%).
- The FCC pipeline slips beyond our central case. A 2.7 GHz auction that misses 2028 would tighten mid-band supply before 2030; a stalled 7 GHz band is already our central case.
The dated events are in the signposts table below.

## Scenarios for 2030

| Scenario | Probability | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Steady grind** | 39% | Spectrum keeps its value, but nobody gets a re-rating. |
| **Supply wave** | 24% | The spectrum law works, supply floods in, and prices per MHz-pop fall. |
| **SpaceX fourth force** | 23% | SpaceX becomes a retail entrant and a deep-pocketed bidder, and value moves from carriers to SpaceX and sellers. |
| **Scarcity premium** | 14% | Demand outruns supply, and existing holders capture the gains. |

### Steady grind (39%)

Defined as: no satellite owner (SpaceX or another) bills 3M or more US mobile lines directly at end-2030, and the median 2029-30 deal price is at least $0.60 but below $1.40 per MHz-pop. Upper C-band clears near $0.65-0.95 ($35-50B), mostly to the three carriers. Traffic roughly doubles to about 300-350 EB, fixed wireless reaches about 29M lines, and revenue per GB keeps falling about 10% a year. SpaceX closes its EchoStar and Grain deals and launches a modest service, but stays a niche operator, and upper mid-band auctions slip past 2030.

- **Spectrum prices:** Mid-band trades near today's range of roughly $0.6-1.4 per MHz-pop. Low-band keeps a premium of about $1.2-2.0 because no new low-band is coming.
- **US carriers:** Revenue grows 2.5-3.5% a year and multiples stay at about 6.5-7.5x. Returns come from dividends and buybacks.
- **Satellite operators:** Satellite-to-phone grows into a few-billion-dollar bundled add-on. SpaceX adds coverage and bids for spectrum, but carriers still set prices.
- **For an investor:** Spectrum exposure pays only through events, such as EchoStar's remainder sale or an Anterix or Array exit. Carrier stocks behave as income holdings.
- **Signposts:** Auction 115 gross bids between $35B and $50B (2027); fixed wireless adds below 3M a year from 2027; SpaceX direct US lines below 1M at end-2029.

### Supply wave (24%)

Defined as: no satellite owner (SpaceX or another) bills 3M or more US mobile lines directly at end-2030, and the median 2029-30 deal price is below $0.60 per MHz-pop. Upper C-band clears low, near $0.40-0.65 ($22-35B), as three carriers split 160 MHz with little contest. NTIA and the FCC move fast: 2.7 GHz in 2028 and a 7 GHz or 4.4 GHz auction by 2030 take new supply past 430 MHz. Traffic growth fades toward 10%, Wi-Fi keeps most phone data, and new auctions clear at $0.3-0.6, echoing Canada's second mid-band tranche.

- **Spectrum prices:** The mid-band deal median falls below $0.60 and keeps falling in real terms. Low-band holds up better at about $1.0-1.6.
- **US carriers:** Capacity gets cheap, which helps margins and fixed wireless, but price competition and cable keep revenue growth near 1.5-2.5%. Multiples sit around 6.5-7x.
- **Satellite operators:** Satellite sharing of carrier spectrum and new auction bands with satellite rights reduce the need to own spectrum. Only the few dedicated satellite bands keep strategic value.
- **For an investor:** Pure-play spectrum holders lose, because sale prices fall and buyers have alternatives. Towers and equipment makers gain more from new deployments than holders do.
- **Signposts:** A favourable NTIA 7 GHz report (10 December 2026) followed by an FCC 7 GHz auction date in 2028 or earlier; Auction 115 gross bids under $35B (2027); a 2.7 GHz auction clearing below $0.50.

### SpaceX fourth force (23%)

Defined as: SpaceX (or another satellite owner) bills at least 3M US mobile lines directly at end-2030, whatever the price level. That is under 1% of US mobile connections, so the damage to carriers here comes mainly from SpaceX's low prices and its bidding for spectrum and towers, not from its market share. SpaceX closes EchoStar and Grain on time, wins meaningful upper C-band blocks, and launches a cheap Starlink Mobile plan in 2028 using satellites, 800 MHz and leased towers. By end-2030 it owns over 100 MHz and keeps buying. Prices rise for low-band and satellite-capable bands, where SpaceX and Amazon bid against carriers, while generic mid-band stays near trend. Carriers answer with price cuts and bundles, so service revenue grows only about 1-2% a year and multiples compress.

- **Spectrum prices:** Prices split into two tiers. Low-band and satellite-usable bands clear at $1.5-2.5 or more, and upper C-band clears above $0.94 about half the time. Generic mid-band stays near $0.7-1.2.
- **US carriers:** Multiples fall toward 5.5-6.5x and the carrier basket lags the S&P 500. Their licences are worth more to others than to them.
- **Satellite operators:** SpaceX is the clear winner, and satellite-to-phone revenue reaches the top of our range ($8-12B). Owners of satellite bands (Amazon-Globalstar, AST's L-band rights) gain strategic value.
- **For an investor:** In this world the gains go to SpaceX, to EchoStar through its SpaceX shares, and to pure plays sold into a bidding contest. Incumbent carriers pay for the shift.
- **Signposts:** SpaceX qualifies for Auction 115 and wins meaningful blocks (2027); a SpaceX plan launches by mid-2028 at an aggressive price; tower deals covering tens of thousands of sites, or a deal to buy a carrier.

### Scarcity premium (14%)

Defined as: no satellite owner (SpaceX or another) bills 3M or more US mobile lines directly at end-2030, and the median 2029-30 deal price is $1.40 per MHz-pop or more. Upper C-band clears above the 2021 price, near $1.00-1.35 ($54-72B), as Verizon, AT&T and a bidding SpaceX fight for blocks next to existing C-band. Traffic re-accelerates on AI uplink, glasses and fixed wireless, with at least one year above 25% growth, while 2.7 GHz and 7 GHz slip. Carriers ration fixed wireless and raise prices, so revenue per GB falls more slowly, and the remaining pure plays sell at premiums.

- **Spectrum prices:** The median deal price is $1.40 or more, with low-band above $2. New auctions clear near or above 2021 levels.
- **US carriers:** Pricing power returns, revenue grows 3-4% a year, and multiples re-rate to about 7.5-8.5x. This is the carriers' best chance of beating the S&P 500.
- **Satellite operators:** Owners of satellite spectrum gain because deep-pocketed buyers compete for their bands. SpaceX stays mainly a wholesale coverage partner to carriers.
- **For an investor:** This is the only world where both carriers and pure plays win. Even here, gains come mostly through sales and auctions, and earnings grow slowly.
- **Signposts:** CTIA traffic growth above 22% in 2026 or 2027; a measured AI traffic share above 1% by 2028; Auction 115 gross bids above $50B (2027); the 2.7 GHz auction slipping to 2029 or later.

**Upper C-band auction price.** Our central estimate is $0.77 per MHz-pop, about $41B of bids for 160 MHz. That is below the lower C-band's own $0.87 on the same 335M-pop basis and close to the 3.45 GHz auction's $0.67 on that basis. Each of the three carriers already holds about 150 MHz or more of mid-band, winners owe about $16B of clearing and altimeter costs on top of their bids (about $0.30 per MHz-pop), and AT&T is stretched after paying $23B for EchoStar spectrum. A serious SpaceX bid is the main upside. The range is wide because US auctions have missed consensus in both directions.

**Upper C-band auction proceeds.** Proceeds are the price forecast times 53.6B MHz-pops (160 MHz times 335M people), so about $41B at the middle. That sits inside Carr's $30-60B and TD Cowen's $30-75B ranges and far below the $81B raised for 280 MHz in 2021. If only the three carriers bid, and they hold back, proceeds land near $30B. If SpaceX bids hard, they land near $50B or more. The $72B top end allows for a surprise like the 2021 auction, which beat its forecast by 55-100%.

**Low- and mid-band deal price, 2029-30.** We expect big 2029-30 deals to have a median near $0.90 per MHz-pop, below the $1.40 AT&T paid EchoStar for a mix of 3.45 GHz and 600 MHz. The likely deal set mixes cheap FCC auctions (2.7 GHz, 1675/2020 MHz, perhaps 4.4 or 7 GHz, at roughly $0.3-0.8) with dearer private sales of low-band and paired spectrum ($1.2-2.5). By 2030 mid-band supply is up about a third, which pushes generic mid-band down while low-band keeps its premium. With only a handful of qualifying deals, which deals happen decides the median, so the range is wide.

**Spectrum deals of $5B or more, 2027-2030.** We expect one spectrum-only deal of $5B or more in 2027-30, with roughly a one-in-three chance of none. The most likely is EchoStar's paired AWS-3 remainder, marked near $8B, which EchoStar has promised to sell by September 2028, though it could be split into smaller pieces. Further buying by SpaceX or Amazon, or a carrier sale or swap after Auction 115, could add one or two more. Before 2025 such deals came about once every five years; the 2025-26 burst came from one forced seller and one new buyer.

**US mobile data traffic in 2030.** We expect about 330 EB in 2030, roughly double 2025's 159 EB, with yearly growth fading from 20% to about 12% (about 15.5% a year on average). Bottom-up, phones at about 37 GB a month give about 150 EB, and about 28M fixed wireless lines at about 520 GB a month give about 175 EB. Mature markets show the same slowdown (UK +18%, Switzerland 20-25%), and 82-89% of phone data still rides Wi-Fi. The top of the range is CTIA's own 22%-a-year path, which industry forecasts have usually overshot.

**US mobile data traffic in 2035.** We extend the 2030 path at about 10% a year, giving about 525 EB in 2035, roughly 3.3 times 2025. Every maturing market shows growth decaying, and Ericsson's own forecast slows to 14% by 2031. The range is wide and skewed up. The low end has growth near 6% after a weak 2030; the high end keeps growth near 16% on uplink-heavy AI devices, 6G and 7 GHz capacity.

**AI share of mobile traffic in 2030.** The only measurement is 0.06% of mobile traffic (Ericsson, April 2025). Reaching our 1.5% median by 2030 needs AI traffic to grow about 120% a year while total traffic grows 15-18%. Text AI is light, much inference runs on the device or server to server, and glasses and indoor robots mostly ride the phone or Wi-Fi. The upper tail is fat because camera and voice assistants and AI video apps could scale the way TikTok did, and the vendors who publish the measurement have reasons to count AI traffic broadly.

**Fixed wireless homes in 2030.** We expect about 29M fixed wireless homes and businesses across the big three at end-2030, up from about 19M in Q3 2026. Net adds have already peaked (3.8M in 2025, about 3.6M in 2026), and we assume they keep decaying toward 2M a year. That lands close to the carriers' own targets: T-Mobile 15M by 2030, Verizon 'well beyond' 8-9M by 2028, AT&T about 5M. New Street's estimate of about 32M of Big 3 capacity caps the upside unless upper C-band adds room.

**Revenue per gigabyte in 2030.** This is our revenue forecast ($266B) divided by our traffic forecast (328 EB), about $0.80 per GB in 2030 against $1.48 in 2025. That is a fall of about 11% a year, half the 2020-25 pace, because traffic growth is slowing while revenue still grows 2-3%. Traffic uncertainty drives nearly all of the range. Even our high case keeps falling every year, so this does not point to a capacity squeeze.

**New spectrum auctioned by 2030.** Upper C-band (160 MHz) is close to certain and sets the floor. Our central case adds the 2.7 GHz band (about 210 MHz), which has approved federal relocation plans and a 2028 FCC target, for about 370 MHz. The 25 MHz 1675/2020 MHz band is roughly a coin flip. The top of the range needs a 7 GHz or 4.4 GHz auction to finish by 2030, which we put at about 30%, because US mid-band auctions usually slip 1-3 years from their first target.

**New spectrum auctioned by 2035.** The 2025 law sets an 800 MHz pipeline with at least 300 MHz auctioned by 2034, and our central case delivers about 640 MHz by 2035. That assumes upper C-band, 2.7 GHz and 7.125-7.4 GHz are done, plus part of 4.4-4.94 GHz about half the time. Statutory spectrum targets are usually met late or in part: about 75% of the 2012 Spectrum Act targets were auctioned within the decade. The law also excludes two big federal bands (3.1-3.45 and 7.4-8.4 GHz), and Defense Department resistance caps the upside.

**Spectrum SpaceX owns in 2030.** SpaceX has about 79 MHz pending: 65 MHz from EchoStar (FCC-approved, closing targeted for 30 November 2027) and 14 MHz of 800 MHz from Grain (awaiting FCC approval). Our median of 88 MHz sits a little above that because at least one modest addition is slightly more likely than not: upper C-band wins (about 45%), EchoStar's paired AWS-3, the 2020-2025 MHz block next to its AWS-4, or 2.7 GHz. The floor near 66 MHz is the case where the Grain deal fails. Musk's description of 800 MHz as the 'last critical piece' keeps the median from going higher.

**Satellite-to-phone revenue in 2030.** Satellite-to-phone revenue is well under $1B today, and we expect about $5B in 2030. Summing by operator: SpaceX wholesale from carrier partners plus any US retail (about $2-2.5B), AST's revenue share with its carrier partners (about $1B), and Apple/Amazon-Globalstar, China and IoT (about $1.5B combined). For scale, the whole legacy satellite-phone industry earns a few billion dollars a year, and carriers mostly bundle satellite service at near-zero extra price. The high end needs broadband-class next-generation service, due from 2027-28, to sell as a paid add-on.

**SpaceX's own US mobile subscribers in 2030.** We expect about 1M US subscribers billed directly by SpaceX at end-2030, with about a one-in-four chance of essentially none. SpaceX has said it plans its own US service once the EchoStar spectrum closes (targeted for 30 November 2027), and we put about 53% on a launch by end-2028. New entrants grow slowly: cable took about 8 years to reach 24M lines with tens of millions of broadband homes to sell to, and Dish/Boost never scaled on its own. The long upper tail covers a cheap plan bundled with Starlink broadband or buying an existing subscriber base.

### Carriers and value capture

**US wireless service revenue in 2030.** We expect about $266B in 2030, up about 2.5% a year from $235B in 2025. Industry revenue has grown about 3% a year in nominal terms since 2007, roughly flat after inflation, even as traffic rose by orders of magnitude. 2026 is firmer (AT&T wireless service +5%, T-Mobile +6-9%), and fixed wireless adds about 0.7 points a year. Cable's free lines, Verizon's falling ARPA and a possible SpaceX price war pull the other way.

**Carrier valuation multiple in 2030.** We expect the big three to trade at about 6.9x forward EBITDA at end-2030, the same as today. US incumbent carriers have mostly traded between 6x and 8x, and today's level is close to that long-run average once T-Mobile's old premium is set aside. The market has punished each SpaceX spectrum step (carriers fell 9-13% on 9 October 2026), and an upper C-band and 6G spending cycle around 2030 weighs on cash flow. A takeover bid or rational three-network pricing could lift it above 8x.

**Cable mobile lines in 2030.** Charter (including Cox) and Comcast had 24.1M mobile lines in Q3 2026 and added about 3.4M over the prior year. We assume adds decay about 10% a year, giving about 35M at end-2030; a straight-line trend gives about 38.6M. Shrinking cable broadband bases cap how many phone lines can be attached, and about half of Comcast's connects are free lines that may churn when promotions end. Cox's footprint inside Charter and the low-cost reseller model on Verizon's network keep growth going.

**US wireless capital spending in 2030.** Industry network capex has been flat at $29-30B since 2023, after the 5G peak of $39B in 2022. We expect about $31.5B in 2030, as upper C-band deployment and early 6G start to lift spending from the trough, plus about 2.5% a year of inflation. Carriers are in harvest mode: Verizon trimmed its 2026 guide, AT&T cut its long-term outlook and T-Mobile guides about $10B. A return toward $39B needs a 2021-22-style spectrum-driven surge, which sits in our top tail.

## Key findings

### Demand

*Undercuts the thesis.* US networks carried 159.3 trillion MB in 2025, up 20%, down from 32% in 2024 and about 35% in 2023. Ericsson measured generative AI apps at 0.06% of one operator's mobile traffic, and Wi-Fi carries 82-89% of US smartphone data. Service revenue per GB fell from $4.50 in 2020 to $1.48 in 2025, so carriers sell more capacity at lower unit prices.

Growth is slowing:
US wireless networks carried 159.3 trillion MB in 2025, according to CTIA. Growth fell to 20% from 32% in 2024 and about 35% in 2023, the sharpest one-year slowdown in the series. Mature markets settle near the same level. UK mobile data grew 18% in 2025.

Ericsson put global traffic above 220 EB a month in Q2 2026, up 23% a year. It forecasts 515 EB a month by 2031, with yearly growth slowing to 14%. Its past forecasts ran high. A 2017-era edition expected North American smartphones to use 48 GB a month in 2023, and the actual figure came in near 26 GB.

Where the new load comes from:
Fixed wireless access (FWA), home broadband sold over licensed spectrum, has absorbed most new US capacity. Analyst Dean Bubley estimated FWA made up about 90% of the 2023 traffic increase. A typical FWA line uses about 500 GB a month, against about 25 GB for a smartphone. Carriers sell this capacity cheaply, which points to spare capacity today.

AI in the traffic data:
- Ericsson measured 21 generative AI apps at 0.06% of one operator's traffic in April 2025. Uplink was 26% of that traffic, against about 10% for traffic overall.
- Waymo ran about 4,000 robotaxis in September 2026. At about 20 GB a day each, that is roughly 0.02% of 2025 US traffic (Whitesail calculation).
- Even 1M robotaxis at that rate would add about 7.3 EB a year, about 4.6% of 2025 US traffic. Licensed wide-area spectrum matters most for outdoor, moving machines such as robotaxis, delivery robots and drones. Smart glasses connect through the phone, and factory robots mostly use Wi-Fi or private 5G. Meta sold more than 7M smart glasses in 2025, and IDC expects 27M display-less units by 2030.
- T-Mobile's Starlink-based T-Satellite service was 0.0002% of its network usage.

Supports the thesis:
- Traffic still compounds at 15-20% a year.
- Ericsson models AI lifting 2031 uplink to 3x (medium adoption) or 5x (high adoption) the 2025 level. Phones transmit with limited power, so uplink-heavy AI uses a large share of spectrum.
- CTIA expects US data demand to roughly quadruple by 2032.
- FWA shows carriers can sell licensed spectrum as broadband.

Cuts against it:
- US growth is slowing, and measured AI traffic is tiny.
- Wi-Fi carries 82-89% of US smartphone data (Opensignal, 2024).
- Service revenue per GB fell from $412 in 2010 to $4.50 in 2020 and $1.48 in 2025, about 20% a year since 2020.
- Verizon trimmed 2026 capex to $16.0-16.5B from $17.0B.
- The forecasts that show an AI step change come mostly from Ericsson, Nokia and CTIA, which all gain from a scarcity story.

Our view:
The demand case is plausible for 2030-2035, mainly through uplink. The data through 2026 does not show it yet. We put about a 14% chance on US growth returning above 25% in any year from 2027 to 2030.

### Substitutes for spectrum

*Undercuts the thesis.* Mid-band spectrum did much of the 5G capacity work, and the FCC's 2010 paper put a sites-only build at $174B against $54B with new spectrum. Since then, US capex fell from $39B in 2022 to about $30B in 2025 while median speeds rose 74% in two years to 334 Mbps, helped by C-band and 3.45 GHz spectrum bought in 2021-22. Real prices for typical unlimited plans fell 10.7% in 2025, and Wi-Fi carries 82-89% of smartphone data.

How capacity gets added:
The standard reading of Cooper's law splits the roughly million-fold gain in wireless capacity since the late 1950s into about 1,600x from smaller cells, about 25x from more spectrum and about 25x from better techniques. Over the long run, densification did most of the work.

The 5G era leaned harder on spectrum. A measurement study of AT&T, T-Mobile and Verizon found that 5G mid-band throughput gains came mainly from wider channels. That is the strongest technical point for the thesis.

What spectrum is worth:
Spectrum is worth what it saves against the next-cheapest option. The FCC's 2010 staff paper estimated a sites-only build at $174B, against $54B with 275 MHz of new spectrum. Three forces hold down that saving through 2035.
- Efficiency gains arrive with normal equipment refresh. Nokia cites 20-40% higher spectral efficiency from 5G-Advanced.
- Demand grows more slowly. Ericsson sees North American data per smartphone rising 13% a year to 2031.
- Supply rises, through the 800 MHz statutory pipeline and possibly 7 GHz.

Wi-Fi is the largest capacity lever by volume. It carried 82-89% of US smartphone data in 2024, and the US kept all 1,200 MHz at 6 GHz for unlicensed use.

Supports the thesis:
- Mid-band spectrum is the cheapest capacity at sites carriers already own.
- The 6G capacity case depends mostly on new spectrum. Ericsson's simulation says 200 MHz at 7 GHz roughly triples downlink capacity on an existing macro grid.
- Demand concentrates in dense cities, where new sites are hardest to add and satellites cannot help.

Cuts against it:
- Shortage forecasts have a poor record. The FCC projected a 275 MHz deficit by 2014, and carriers absorbed the growth with LTE, cell splitting and Wi-Fi. Brattle's study for CTIA now projects about 400 MHz by 2027 and about 1,400 MHz by 2032.
- Industry capex fell from a $39B peak in 2022 to about $30B in 2025.
- The combined median download speed of the three national carriers reached 334 Mbps in H1 2026, up 74% in two years. Much of that came from C-band and 3.45 GHz spectrum bought in 2021-22, so it shows spare capacity today.
- Real prices for typical unlimited plans fell 10.7% in 2025, and price per GB fell more than 21%.
- Verizon says its C-band build is about 90% complete and its FWA targets need no extra capital.

Our view:
If spectrum were short, carriers would be raising prices and spending more on their networks. Instead, price per GB is falling and capex is flat. We put 7% on revenue per GB rising in any year to 2030 and 14% on a year of traffic growth above 25%, our two tests for a capacity crunch. Our central case has upper C-band bids slightly below 2021's like-for-like price, with no broad re-rating of spectrum.

### Supply and rules

*Mixed for the thesis.* The 2025 budget law sets an 800 MHz pipeline, and 160 MHz of upper C-band goes to auction on April 27, 2027. We estimate about 370 MHz of new licensed spectrum auctioned by 2030 and about 640 MHz by 2035, roughly 55% more than today's base of about 1,155 MHz. Low-band is the exception, with almost nothing new coming below 1 GHz.

What exists today:
The FCC's spectrum screen, its trigger for closer deal review, stands at 385 MHz. The screen is set at about one-third of suitable spectrum, which implies about 1,155 MHz of licensed sub-6 GHz mobile spectrum. AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile hold most of it. After buying EchoStar's licences, AT&T reaches 405 MHz in some counties, above the trigger. The FCC still approved the deal with conditions, and it blocked no 2025-2026 deal.

The pipeline:
- The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (July 2025) restored FCC auction authority through September 30, 2034 and set an 800 MHz pipeline. The FCC must auction at least 300 MHz, and at least 500 MHz must come from federal spectrum between 1.3 and 10.5 GHz.
- Upper C-band (160 MHz, Auction 115) starts bidding on April 27, 2027, with 3,248 licences. It creates a contiguous 440 MHz C-band range.
- FCC Chairman Brendan Carr targets a 2.7 GHz auction of about 210 MHz in 2028.
- NTIA's study of 7.125-7.4 GHz (275 MHz) is due December 10, 2026.
- The law excludes 3.1-3.45 GHz and 7.4-8.4 GHz.

Our central estimate is about 370 MHz of new licensed spectrum auctioned by 2030 and about 640 MHz by 2035.

Sharing grows too:
In October 2026 the FCC proposed adding 482 MHz to its Supplemental Coverage from Space framework, taking eligible bands from 5 to 13. The framework lets satellites reuse carriers' spectrum. AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile signed a joint venture on October 1, 2026 to pool spectrum for satellite partners.

Supports the thesis:
- Low-band is structurally scarce. No meaningful new supply below 1 GHz is in the pipeline.
- Ownership is concentrated, and the screen limits who can buy. That protects the value of spectrum already held.
- GSMA puts North America's 2035-2040 mid-band need at 2.7-3.8 GHz, against about 1 GHz today.

Cuts against it:
- Mid-band supply grows about a third by 2030 and about 55% by 2035 if the law is followed.
- The US mid-band gap is disputed. PolicyTracker counts 1-7 GHz spectrum assigned to operators at 791 MHz in the US and 835 MHz in China.
- Carriers signal restraint. Verizon says upper C-band takes four to five years to deploy. T-Mobile says it will buy only where spectrum beats densifying its network.
- Sharing rules reduce the need to own spectrum outright.

Our view:
A scarcity premium most likely holds in low-band and in rights usable by both satellites and ground networks. It most likely shrinks in generic mid-band.

### Prices and deals

*Mixed for the thesis.* SpaceX agreed to pay a reported $8B for Grain's 800 MHz licences, about $1.73 per MHz-pop and about 2.2x the $3.6B in cash and licences Grain paid two months earlier. AT&T paid about $23B for EchoStar's 3.45 GHz and 600 MHz licences, about $1.40 per MHz-pop. Wide mid-band blocks still clear lower at auction. Lower C-band cleared at about $0.94 in 2021, and TD Cowen's $30-75B range for upper C-band implies $0.56-1.40.

Recent private deals:
- T-Mobile sold its 800 MHz licences to Grain Management for $2.9B in cash plus $690M of 600 MHz licences, closing August 10, 2026. That is about $0.78 per MHz-pop. T-Mobile keeps 50% of Grain's later gains above a set return.
- On October 8, 2026, SpaceX agreed to buy the same licences, up to 14 MHz paired. The Wall Street Journal reported about $8B in cash. New Street puts that at about $1.73 per MHz-pop.
- AT&T closed its roughly $23B purchase of EchoStar's 3.45 GHz and 600 MHz licences on July 28, 2026, about $1.40 per MHz-pop (CreditSights). New Street said the price was about $9B above EchoStar's cost.
- SpaceX agreed to pay $17B for EchoStar's AWS-4 and H-block licences, about $1.03 per MHz-pop (MoffettNathanson), and $2.6B in stock for unpaired AWS-3, about $0.52, both payable at the closing targeted for 30 November 2027.

FCC auctions:
- AWS-3 (2015) raised $44.9B; the paired blocks averaged about $2.7 per MHz-pop (about $2.2 across all 65 MHz), the US peak.
- Lower C-band (2021) raised $81.17B at about $0.94. AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile paid about 96% of it.
- 3.45 GHz (2022) raised $22.5B at about $0.74 as reported (about $0.67 on today's 335M population).
- The AWS-3 re-auction closed on June 23, 2026 at $3.57B. New Street put the average at $2.53 per MHz-pop, and Verizon paid about 88% of the total.
- TD Cowen expects $30-75B for upper C-band in 2027, about $0.56-1.40 per MHz-pop. Winners also pay about $16B of satellite and altimeter costs.

Supports the thesis:
- The 800 MHz price more than doubled in two months.
- EchoStar sold to AT&T, and agreed to sell to SpaceX, at large premiums to its cost.
- Amazon agreed to pay about $11.57B for Globalstar, largely for its satellite spectrum rights.
- Anterix raised its own estimate of its 900 MHz spectrum to $2.5B-$7B+ after the FCC widened the band.
- Small amounts of paired lower mid-band with mature phone support still fetch $2.5-3.0 per MHz-pop.

Cuts against it:
- Wide mid-band blocks sell for far less per MHz-pop than the 2015 peak, and the upper C-band range sits near lower C-band.
- The buyer pool is tiny. Real competition appeared only when a fourth party showed up. SpaceX accounts for about $27.6B, roughly 45-50%, of 2025-26 private deal value, and Musk called the 800 MHz licences the last critical piece for complete US phone coverage, though an urban service would still need more mid-band capacity.
- mmWave sits near $0.01 per MHz-pop, and the 2.5 GHz leftover auction raised $428M against $1-5B expected.
- The FCC attaches conditions, such as an escrow and faster buildout on AT&T-EchoStar.

Our view:
Our central estimate for upper C-band is about $41B, or about $0.77 per MHz-pop (80% range $0.42-1.35). We expect low-band private prices to hold at $1.2-2.0 by 2030 and upper mid-band to trend to $0.5-1.0.

### Satellites and SpaceX

*Mixed for the thesis.* SpaceX, valued at about $1.77T at its June 2026 IPO, has agreed deals for about 79 MHz of US spectrum usable on the ground, including EchoStar's 65 MHz and Grain's 800 MHz. Starlink Mobile reported about 22M monthly users in Q3 2026, but SpaceX does not disclose direct-to-device revenue. By our estimates satellites carry about three orders of magnitude less traffic per square km than city towers, so convergence adds a bidder and a coverage layer and leaves urban capacity to ground networks.

Where direct-to-device stands:
SpaceX's first-generation Direct to Cell fleet has about 650 satellites. In the US it runs on about 5 MHz of T-Mobile spectrum and carries texts, some app data and limited voice. Starlink Mobile reached about 22M monthly users in Q3 2026, up 37% on Q2. Connectivity revenue was $5.25B of SpaceX's $14.6B in Q3 2026, and direct-to-device is a small, unreported slice of it. T-Mobile's T-Satellite service was 0.0002% of its network usage.

SpaceX's spectrum build:
- The FCC approved the transfer of EchoStar's AWS-4 (40 MHz), H-block (10 MHz) and unpaired AWS-3 (15 MHz) on May 12, 2026, with rights for ground, satellite or hybrid use. The final closing is targeted for November 30, 2027.
- SpaceX agreed on October 8, 2026 to buy Grain's nationwide 800 MHz, up to 14 MHz paired. The FCC accepted the filing on November 10, and petitions are due December 1.
- SpaceX says it needs 800 MHz because its 2 GHz spectrum does not reach indoors.
- The FCC authorized up to 15,000 next-generation direct-to-device satellites in October 2026. SpaceX targets about 20x the per-satellite throughput of the first generation.

Rivals are consolidating. Amazon is buying Globalstar for about $11.57B, and Rocket Lab is buying Iridium at an enterprise value of about $8.0B. AST SpaceMobile has 12 satellites operating and reported Q3 2026 revenue of $42.3M.

The physics:
Analyst estimates put Starlink's broadband capacity at 4-55 Mbps per square km, and we estimate next-generation satellite-to-phone capacity at about 1-6 Mbps. By our estimates, a dense urban macro grid delivers about 1,500-9,000 Mbps per square km. A satellite spreads its capacity over a huge area and cannot reuse spectrum as tightly as towers 0.5-2 km apart. That is why SpaceX buys low-band and, per Bloomberg, seeks access to at least 80,000 US cell sites. TD Cowen puts a ground network build above $80B.

Supports the thesis:
- Convergence has brought a new bidder with deep pockets. SpaceX's IPO raised $75B ($85.7B with the over-allotment), and it can pay partly in its own stock.
- SpaceX agreed to pay about $1.03 per MHz-pop for AWS-4 and H-block, a band EchoStar could not use for over a decade, payable at the closing targeted for 30 November 2027.
- The Globalstar and Iridium deals repriced satellite spectrum.
- SpaceX's move into 800 MHz confirms low-band as the scarce input.

Cuts against it:
- Direct-to-device cannot serve dense urban demand, where carriers earn most of their money.
- Revenue is small and mostly undisclosed.
- The FCC adds usable supply through flexible-use waivers and sharing rules.
- SpaceX's appetite may fade once it closes on about 79 MHz.
- Carrier stocks fell on each SpaceX spectrum step.

Our view:
We put about 85% on direct-to-device carrying under 2% of developed-market mobile data in 2035. New buyers reprice spectrum. Incumbent holders gain little of that through pricing.

### Who captures the value

*Undercuts the thesis.* AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile fell 9.8%, 8.8% and 13.3% on October 9, 2026, the day after SpaceX agreed to buy Grain's 800 MHz. Verizon added 406,000 postpaid phones in Q3 2026 while its ARPA fell 1.9%, and CTIA's wireless service price index fell 4.1% in 2025. EchoStar, a seller with two bidders, posted the best shareholder return in the Fortune 500.

Pricing:
US carriers sell more capacity each year at lower unit prices. CTIA's service price index fell 4.1% in 2025, and price per GB fell 21%. Service revenue per connection was $33.14 a month in 2025, against $49.26 in 2007.

Q3 2026 results:
- Verizon added 406,000 postpaid phones, against 44,000 a year earlier. Its postpaid ARPA fell 1.9% to $168.08.
- AT&T added 379,000 phones and grew wireless service revenue 5.0% to $17.76B.
- T-Mobile's ARPA rose 4% to $155.73, so it is the one carrier showing pricing power. Its account adds fell 31% to 274,000.
- Charter added 508,000 mobile lines and Comcast 368,000. Together that beats AT&T's and Verizon's combined phone adds. About half of Comcast's postpaid connects were free lines.

Stock reactions:
The market treats SpaceX's spectrum buying as bad news for incumbents. Carriers fell 4-5% when EchoStar agreed to sell to SpaceX in September 2025. On October 9, 2026, after the Grain deal, AT&T fell 9.8%, Verizon 8.8% and T-Mobile 13.3%. MoffettNathanson notes the three carriers still hold about 14-15 times as much spectrum as SpaceX.

Where the gains went:
EchoStar shares rose about 375% in 2025 and about 430% from a near-distressed level in January 2025 to June 2026, the best return in the Fortune 500. It sold to AT&T, and agreed to sell to SpaceX, under FCC pressure. Carriers carry licences at historical cost. T-Mobile's spectrum licences were $99.98B at September 30, 2026, about 41% of its enterprise value (Whitesail calculation). Higher resale prices reach carrier earnings mainly when carriers sell, as T-Mobile will through its 50% share of Grain's gain on the resale to SpaceX, and they rarely sell.

Supports the thesis:
- Holders capture value when an outside bidder appears, as EchoStar and Grain did.
- The move from four networks to three, plus SpaceX's need for towers, opens options for wholesale deals, tower leases or M&A. Morgan Stanley called the Grain deal constructive for tower companies.

Cuts against it:
- Spectrum in a new entrant's hands hurts incumbents' competitive position.
- Cable takes share with free lines while reselling Verizon's network.
- Carrier returns come from lower capex and higher free cash flow. T-Mobile guides 2026 adjusted free cash flow of $18.7-18.8B.
- No 2026 carrier result shows AI devices moving service revenue.

Our view:
Our 2030 scenarios put 39% on a steady grind, 24% on a supply wave that lowers prices, 23% on SpaceX becoming a fourth national force and 14% on a scarcity premium. Only the scarcity premium lifts carriers to about 7.5-8.5x EBITDA.

### Past spectrum bets

*Undercuts the thesis.* Of 16 companies whose main asset was spectrum, 3 clearly won, 8 lost and 5 were mixed. Dish/EchoStar, the most relevant case, turned more than $30B of licence spending into about $42.6B of sales at deal values, about 1.4x cash cost over roughly ten years. With the 261.8M SpaceX shares it is due marked at the 13 November close instead of the $42.40 deal price, proceeds are about $68B, about 2.3x. Wins took 4-14 years, while losses usually showed within 2-5.

The record:
We reviewed 26 cases from 1996 to 2026. They include government auctions and separate pieces of the Dish programme, so treat the tally as a rough guide. 6 clearly made money, 12 lost most of the capital or, where a government was selling, left most spectrum unsold, and 8 were mixed or pending. Among the 16 companies whose main asset was spectrum, 3 won, 8 lost and 5 were mixed. A holder who bought at market typically ended between break-even and a loss after financing and carrying costs.

Dish/EchoStar, 2008-2026:
- Dish invested more than $30B in licences, including more than $10B through designated entities. Its books carried them at about $37.5B with capitalized interest.
- It sold 3.45 GHz and 600 MHz to AT&T for about $23B (closed July 2026) and agreed to sell AWS-4 and H-block to SpaceX for about $17B and unpaired AWS-3 for about $2.6B in stock (closing targeted for November 2027).
- Headline proceeds of about $42.6B are about 1.4x cash cost, or roughly 3.5-4% a year. Against the $37.5B book basis, the multiple is about 1.1x. These use the $42.40 deal price for the 261.8M SpaceX shares; marked at the 13 November close of $139.11, proceeds are about $68B, about 2.3x cash cost.
- The best piece was the 2 GHz licences, bought out of the TerreStar and DBSD bankruptcies for about $2.86B, plus H-block for $1.67B. They sold for $17B at deal value, about 3.8x over 13-14 years, and more with the SpaceX shares marked to market. The FCC's 2012 decision to allow ground use, and a satellite buyer, made that happen.
- Two Dish subsidiaries filed for Chapter 11 on June 30, 2026. EchoStar sold under FCC pressure with debt coming due.

Other cases:
- NextWave bid $4.74B for PCS licences in 1996, went bankrupt, and sold them for about $5.4B in 2004-05, about 1.1x after nine years.
- Clearwire's backers put in $3.2B in 2008. Google lost 94% on its $500M stake. Sprint bought out the rest in 2013 at $5 a share.
- Harbinger put about $3B into LightSquared. The FCC froze its plan over GPS interference, and Harbinger's main fund lost 47% in 2011.
- Straight Path sold to Verizon for $3.1B in 2017 after a bidding war with AT&T. The Treasury took 20% of proceeds, and the parties paid about $614M to settle with the FCC.

Supports the thesis:
- A second motivated bidder was the best single predictor of a good exit, and SpaceX now plays that role.
- AWS-4 shows how satellite-ground convergence can create value.

Cuts against it:
- Even the best recent case returned single digits a year across the whole programme.
- Treasuries, operators and creditors took much of the upside.
- Bets failed on interference (LightSquared), technology shifts (WiMAX), oversupply (mmWave), peak-price bidding (European 3G) and carrying costs (Dish, Ligado).

Our view:
A new pure-play spectrum position bought at market has about a 25-35% chance of returning more than 1.5x by 2030 and about a 25-30% chance of ending below 0.5x. FCC rule changes and the number of motivated bidders drive outcomes more than total data demand.

### Outside the US

*Undercuts the thesis.* GSMA finds operator revenue per MHz fell about 60% from 2014 to 2023, even as spectrum costs rose to about 7% of revenue. Analysys Mason puts 49 European 3.3-3.9 GHz auctions at an average of €0.067 per MHz-pop, while US C-band cleared at about $0.94, roughly 12-13 times higher. India sold only about 141 MHz of the 10,523 MHz it offered in 2024.

Prices per unit are falling:
GSMA's 2025 study of about 250 operators finds revenue per MHz fell about 60% between 2014 and 2023. Operators hold about 80% more spectrum than in 2014, so total spectrum costs rose from about 4.5% to 7.4% of revenue. GSMA lobbies for lower prices, and the auction data point the same way.
- Analysys Mason averages 49 national 3.3-3.9 GHz auctions from 2018 to 2024 at €0.067 per MHz-pop.
- Italy's 2018 auction, at about $0.42, was the high point. Germany (2019) cleared at about $0.19, and the UK (2018) and France (2020) at about $0.15.
- In Canada, Rogers paid C$3.38 per MHz-pop for 3500 MHz in 2021 and C$0.32 for 3800 MHz in 2023, about 90% less.
- Ofcom sold all 5.4 GHz of UK mmWave in October 2025 for £39m in total.
- NERA puts the global three-year mid-band average at $0.17 per MHz-pop.

Unsold spectrum:
India's June 2024 auction sold about 141 MHz of roughly 10,523 MHz offered. It raised ₹11,340 crore against a reserve value of ₹96,238 crore, about 12%. In February 2026 India's regulator recommended offering all 11,790 MHz at lower reserve prices.

Policy moves away from cash:
- Germany extended 800, 1800 and 2600 MHz rights by five years in March 2025 instead of auctioning them.
- Brazil's 2021 auction turned about 90% of the spectrum's value into investment obligations.
- GSMA says low-cost renewals could save European operators up to €30bn of €104bn in 2025-2035 spectrum costs.

The US is the outlier:
US lower C-band cleared at about $0.94 per MHz-pop in 2021, roughly 12-13 times the European average. Licence terms differ, so the comparison is approximate. Three national bidders, higher revenue per user and shortages at the time explain much of the gap.

Supports the thesis:
- Holdings and total spending keep rising.
- Satellite-usable bands are scarce and contested. The EU plans a single selection for the 2 GHz satellite band, whose licences expire in May 2027.

Cuts against it:
- Prices per MHz-pop fall as new mid-band arrives.
- Large shares of offered spectrum go unsold.
- Politics caps who controls satellite bands. The EU proposal effectively reserves two of three 2 GHz blocks for European users, so a non-European operator such as SpaceX could win at most one.
- Analysys Mason says operators may value 6-8 GHz spectrum only in high-traffic areas.

Our view:
We put about 10-15% on spectrum broadly becoming far more valuable outside the US. Any premium is more likely in satellite-capable bands and in the US.

### Ways to invest

*Undercuts the thesis.* AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile own most US spectrum, but their shares track pricing power and fell 9-13% after SpaceX agreed to buy Grain's 800 MHz. EchoStar now mainly holds a claim on 261.8M SpaceX shares plus $8.5B of cash due at closing. Small pure plays such as Anterix and NextNav pay off mainly through a sale to one of a handful of buyers.

The carriers:
AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile own most of the valuable bands. Licences make up a large share of their enterprise value, about 41% at T-Mobile on book value (Whitesail calculation). They are also the main buyers, so scarcity raises what they pay for capacity about as much as it lifts their asset values. Their shares fell 9-13% the day after SpaceX agreed to buy Grain's 800 MHz. We put about 36% on them beating the S&P 500 from November 2026 to the end of 2030.

EchoStar (ECHO):
EchoStar sold or agreed to sell its main licences for about $42.6B at deal values. It is due 261.8M SpaceX shares, fixed at $42.40 each when the deal was signed (about $11.1B) and worth about $36B at SpaceX's $139.11 close on 13 November, plus $8.5B of cash at the final closing, targeted for November 2027. It held $13.44B of cash at September 30, 2026. UBS valued the SpaceX stake at about $39B. The stock now trades on SpaceX's share price, closing risk and taxes.

SpaceX (SPCX):
SpaceX listed in June 2026 at $135 a share and traded near $137 in mid-November. Its roughly $27.6B of agreed spectrum purchases ($19.6B from EchoStar and a reported ~$8B for Grain's 800 MHz) is about 1.5% of its value. Owning SPCX is a bet on Starlink, launch and AI. The main 180-day lockup ends on December 8, 2026.

Small pure plays:
- Anterix (900 MHz) raised its own estimate of its spectrum value to $2.5B-$7B+ after the FCC widened the band to 10 MHz. It sells licences to utilities in small deals, such as $13.0M to CPS Energy.
- NextNav (lower 900 MHz) depends on an FCC petition that faces heavy opposition.
- Globalstar and Iridium have agreed takeovers, which caps their upside. Amazon pays $90 a share for Globalstar, and Rocket Lab pays $54 for Iridium.
- Array keeps its C-band after selling other spectrum to AT&T and Verizon.

Satellite names and funds:
AST SpaceMobile rose 244% in 2025 and remains a high-risk bet on execution. No fund isolates spectrum. IYZ is led by the large carriers; XTL gives equal weight to carriers, equipment makers and small spectrum and satellite names. We did not verify either fund's holdings. XOVR and DXYZ hold SpaceX, which now trades directly.

Supports the thesis:
- Unused spectrum sells at scale. EchoStar's shares rose about 430% from January 2025 to June 2026.
- SpaceX keeps buying spectrum it can use on the ground.

Cuts against it:
- The 2025-26 deal prices sit within past auction ranges.
- Spectrum is illiquid and allocated by regulators. AT&T's EchoStar deal took 11 months to close, and SpaceX's will take more than two years if it closes as targeted.
- Pure plays are small and each depends on one sale.

Our view:
The listed names closest to the theme are the small pure plays, ECHO and the carrier funds. The pure plays depend on sale timing, ECHO on SpaceX's share price, and the carrier funds on carrier pricing.

## Method and caveats

**Research.** Nine researchers each covered one part of the market: demand, substitutes for spectrum, supply and rules, prices and deals, satellites, carrier economics, listed exposure, past spectrum bets and markets outside the US. They gathered 379 findings, each with a source link and a tag: P for regulators, filings and official statistics, S for reputable press and analysts, W for directional sources. Our environment could not open some primary sites (SEC, FCC, Reuters) directly, so those facts rest on search extracts of the documents.

**Independent check.** Separate fact-checkers re-searched the 102 key claims with differently worded searches. 92 were confirmed, 5 were corrected (the corrections are applied), 5 could not be confirmed independently, and none was refuted. Each finding's check result is in the Appendix of the interactive report. A second review read the finished report for accuracy, reasoning and clarity, and its corrections are applied.

**Forecasts.** We wrote 32 questions with resolution criteria: 18 quantities and 14 yes-or-no events. Five forecasting lenses answered every question separately: a reference-class lens, a bottom-up model, an advocate for the thesis, a skeptic, and a policy and market-structure lens. We take the median central estimate, the average 10th and 90th percentiles and, for yes-or-no questions, the geometric mean of the odds. A chief forecaster then checked the numbers for consistency and wrote the four scenarios.

**How to read the ranges.** Each range is an 80% interval, so we expect the outcome to fall outside it about one time in five. The ticks on each forecast show where each lens put its central estimate. The lenses agreed closely, partly because they worked from the same research, so treat the ranges as careful judgments; no statistical model produced them. On the variables that matter most for the thesis, the panel sat below the research team's own medians: AI share of traffic 1.5% against 4%, satellite-to-phone revenue $4.8B against $6B, SpaceX direct US lines 1M against 3M, and 6G by 2030 49% against 60%. The panel leaned on slowing measured data and vendors' record of overshooting. At 3M SpaceX lines, the SpaceX fourth force scenario would be close to even odds.

Risks to these forecasts. Interest rates: spectrum is a long-lived asset, and higher rates cut auction budgets and carrier multiples, while lower rates do the reverse. Policy: a change in FCC leadership after the 2028 election, political friction around SpaceX approvals, Defense Department resistance or litigation could slow the pipeline, which would tighten supply, or change ownership limits. Technology: an on-time 6G standard raises demand for 7 GHz but also adds supply, while Wi-Fi 7 on 6 GHz and on-device AI keep moving traffic off licensed networks. Each could shift the scenario weights by several points. 

Forecasts are judgments made on 20 November 2026 and will be wrong in places; the signposts say what would change them.
